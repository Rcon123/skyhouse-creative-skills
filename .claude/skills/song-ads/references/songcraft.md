# Songcraft: making ad copy sing without losing the ad

The bar: when this plays out loud, it sounds like a real song someone wrote, with rhymes the listener can HEAR. Fidelity lives in the beats, claims, and signature phrases, not in sentence structure. A verse that rhymes cleanly while keeping the ad's meaning and money words is more faithful than a verbatim verse that sounds like someone reading an ad over music.

## The minimal-edit hierarchy

Work down this list line by line. Stop at the first level that makes the line genuinely sing, and never make an edit that does not improve how the line sings (a lateral reword loses fidelity and buys nothing; keep it verbatim or improve it). Every level below 1 gets an entry in the change log.

1. **Keep the line verbatim.** Only when the line already scans, or it is delivered spoken. Verbatim lines that leave a SUNG section unrhymed and unmetered are not fidelity, they are mess; move them to a spoken slot or work down the hierarchy.
2. **Trim filler.** Cut "See,", "Not gonna lie,", "You know the ones", "apparently", "literally" only when the line is too long to breathe. These are Ryan-voice connective tissue, so trim, do not purge.
3. **Reorder inside the line.** Moving a phrase to the end can land a rhyme with zero word changes ("I showed her my purse instead" vs "instead I showed her my purse").
4. **Swap one word at the line end** for a near-synonym that rhymes or scans. One word, at the end, only when the line carries a rhyme slot.
5. **Add a small connective word** (and, so, but, now, oh) to fix meter. Cheap and invisible.
6. **Rewrite the line.** Last resort, chorus only if possible, always flagged in the change log with the original beside it.

## Rhyme: the rules that protect the copy

- **Every sung section commits to a rhyme scheme, no exceptions.** Unrhymed lines belong in spoken slots only. The listener should be able to hear the rhyme land on first listen; that landing is what makes it register as a song.
- **Commit each verse to one vowel family** (long e: me/see/be/please; long i: eyes/time/light/try; long a: day/way/fade/face). Stacking a family across a verse's rhyme slots gives even slant rhymes an audible, satisfying pattern, and it multiplies your options for keeping the ad's own words.
- **ABCB is the default scheme for verses.** Only lines 2 and 4 need to rhyme, so the odd lines can stay closest to verbatim. AABB doubles the rhyme burden and doubles the edits; use it only when the ad's own words hand it to you.
- **Slant rhyme beats perfect rhyme.** purse/hurt, mirror/nearer, fade/face, name/again all count. Modern country, pop, and hip-hop run almost entirely on slant rhyme; forcing perfect rhyme is what makes song ads sound like jingles from 1994.
- **Mine the ad's own vocabulary for rhymes before importing new words.** List the line-end words the ad already contains and look for pairs (be/me/naturally/see live in almost every skincare ad). An imported rhyme word is a level-4 edit; a found one is free.
- **Never bend a claim to land a rhyme.** If the rhyme needs the product to do more than the ad said, kill the rhyme, keep the claim.
- **Choruses may rhyme harder** (AABB or AAAA on an open vowel) because they repeat and need to hook. Long-e and long-o vowel families are the easiest to stack and the best to sing.

## Prosody: making lines land on the beat

- **Match syllable counts within a section, roughly.** Paired lines within +/-2 syllables of each other get the same melody from Suno. Wildly uneven pairs force it to cram or drag.
- **Stressed syllables carry the melody.** Read the line aloud; the naturally stressed words should be the important ones (product, benefit, emotion). If the stress falls on "the" or "of," reorder (level 3).
- **One breath per line, 4-8 words.** Split long ad sentences across lines at the natural pause; splitting is a level-1 move, it changes nothing.
- **End emphasized or held lines on open vowels** (ah, oh, ay, ee). Lines ending in consonant clusters (asked, striped, months) die when held; put those words mid-line.
- **Short punch lines are gold.** "Her jaw dropped." "I was desperate." Leave them alone and give them their own line; Suno hits them.

## The spoken-word weapon

The single best fidelity tool. A `[Spoken Word]` section or a `(spoken)` line delivers ad copy VERBATIM with zero rhyme or meter obligation, and the talk-verse-into-sung-chorus format is exactly the story-song ad trend. Use it for:

- The cold-open hook (the ad's first line, word for word, before the music fully kicks in)
- Punch lines and reveals ("Her jaw dropped.")
- The CTA outro (sung CTAs sound like jingles; spoken CTAs sound like a friend)
- Any beat where every word is load-bearing (the mechanism, the credible source)

Do not exceed roughly a third of the song spoken, or it stops being a song.

## Structure: the song walks the ad top to bottom

The ad's beat order is engineered (hook, problem, failed solutions, credible-source discovery, skepticism, transformation, payoff, CTA) and the song follows it exactly. Cut sections at the ad's natural beat boundaries: each beat becomes a verse, the skepticism-into-transformation beat makes a natural bridge, the CTA is the spoken outro.

The hard rule this protects: **no beat may surface earlier in the song than the ad reveals it.** A standard pop structure drops the chorus after verse 1, which for a story ad means blurting the mechanism or payoff before the story earns it. That destroys the ad. Story ads use a late-first-chorus structure instead, and that is a real, proven song form (the classic story ballad); do not apologize for it with an early chorus.

## Chorus construction

- The chorus is the ad's own payoff or summary lines ("It wasn't just about looking younger, it was about feeling like myself again"), tightened into 2-4 lines. Nothing invented, nothing pulled forward from a later beat, and the mechanism stays in its discovery slot in the verses.
- **First appearance is exactly where those lines sit in the ad.** Usually that is two-thirds of the way in, right after the transformation lands. From there it may repeat to the end (repeat after the payoff verse, repeat to close). Repetition after first appearance is fine; backfilling it earlier is not.
- Identical text every repeat: repetition is what makes Suno give it the same big melody and what makes the song feel like a song.
- Repeating one of Ryan's own lines inside the chorus ("feeling like myself again, oh, feeling like myself again") is a free way to give a short chorus musical weight without inventing anything.

## Compression rules

- Target 200-280 words of lyric for a 2.5-3.5 minute song. Cut down from the ad by REMOVING whole beats, not by rewording all of them into mush.
- Cut order (first to go): repeated restatements of the same emotion, second examples of failed solutions, decorative details that do not advance the story.
- Never cut: the hook scene, the credible source, the skepticism beat, the mechanism claim, the transformation, the CTA.
- A 60-90 second short version cuts to: hook scene, one problem line, discovery, chorus x2, spoken CTA.

## Older audiences (60+): clarity is the whole game

Most of Ryan's offers target 60+. They must hear and follow every word, and comprehension is what sells. Defaults for this audience:

- Slow to mid tempo. Fast phrasing loses them.
- Vocals upfront in the mix, clear enunciation, minimal reverb. Put "clear enunciation" and "vocals upfront" style descriptors in the style prompt.
- Sparse instrumentation under the verses (acoustic guitar, soft piano); the band can swell in the chorus but never over the vocal.
- Exclude heavy drums, autotune, and anything that smears the vocal.
- Simple short words, steady meter, strong audible rhyme. Rhyme is not decoration for this audience; it is a comprehension aid. Rhyme and repetition tell an older listener where the line ends and what mattered in it.
- Classic country ballad, easy-listening pop, or gospel-tinged styles read as "my music" to 65+; trend-chasing production reads as noise.

## Genre selection

The ad's narrator is the singer. Choose the genre they would plausibly star in:

- Woman 45+ story ad (beauty, health, family stakes): classic country or heartland storytelling ballad, warm mature female vocal, conversational verses, big emotional chorus.
- Man 40+ (T-boost, gut, back pain): country rock or outlaw country, gravelly male vocal.
- Younger DTC / trendy product: pop or indie-pop, bright vocal, faster tempo.
- Comedic or absurd angle: the joke is playing it STRAIGHT. An earnest, beautiful ballad about a prostate flush is funnier and more shareable than a novelty track. Never pick "funny" production.

Decide, state the pick with one alternate, keep moving.
