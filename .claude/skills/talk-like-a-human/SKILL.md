# Talk like a human (Ryan's own lesson, 2026-11-03)

Three rounds of my ProDenta script read like copy. He stopped and wrote the opening himself, then graded five more rounds that drifted back. This file is that lesson. His text is the bar; measure every draft against it before he sees it.

## 1. The bar, verbatim (Benny, ProDenta, 11-03)

> And no I didn't put him under. Thank God.
>
> The craziest thing is it also wasn't from brushing his teeth, or Greenies, or water additives, or anything like that.
>
> I still can't believe this actually worked.
>
> Because just a few months ago, Benny's breath was so bad and his teeth were so caked with tartar that I didn't even want him near my face anymore.
>
> I still feel so ashamed admitting this, but I let him sit there in pain for almost a year because I was terrified of putting him under for a cleaning at 13.
>
> So I kept putting it off, and I'd lie in bed at night feeling like the worst dog mom in the world.
>
> Thank God for my friend Carol, though, because she completely changed everything. She's been a vet tech for 20 years, and she sent me this article from America's top vet who said that those cleanings don't actually touch ###

Paragraph word counts: 6, 22, 7, 30, 31, 22, 35. The short ones are whole spoken sentences with a feeling in them, never a clipped line for punch.

## 2. What his version does that mine did not

1. **One idea per paragraph, 12 to 35 words, a blank line between.** Over 40 words is "chunky" even when it flows. Under 8 words is a fragment unless it is a whole thought with a feeling in it ("Thank God." "I still can't believe this actually worked.").
2. **Sentences run together the way a person talks.** Because, so, though, and, but, or anything like that. A paragraph is usually one sentence with its connectives in it, sometimes two. Never three short sentences in a row ("She's 13. She has every tooth. She has never been put under." = "really choppy, not the right way to talk").
3. **The feeling is said out loud, in plain words.** Thank God. I still feel so ashamed admitting this. The craziest thing is. I'd lie in bed at night feeling like the worst dog mom in the world. Never a gesture standing in for a feeling, never a fact about her role ("he drove her to rehab" is not emotion; "the most painful thing in my life" is).
4. **The hardest fact comes first, right after the discovery.** "She sent me this article from America's top vet who said that those cleanings don't actually touch [the thing rotting his teeth]." Never "who said something I'd never heard in my life" (Ryan: "that's so vague, you're going backwards"). The clause after "said that" is the most interesting thing the article says.
5. **Every line can be placed by a stranger.** No pronoun without a name in reach, no "this" without the thing, no claim without what it is a claim about. "The 5 healthy foods were why" fails because why what. Say it whole: the 5 healthy foods are why her number never comes down.
6. **Say exactly what she did.** Made an appointment, took him to the vet, lifted his lip. Never "I took her in" or "I let him stare" or "the crook of my knees". If the picture lives in the writer's head, spell it out.
7. **Helpers arrive with a reason she says out loud.** "Thank God for my friend Carol, though, because she completely changed everything. She's been a vet tech for 20 years, and she sent me..." Never a dropped credential line ("My niece Katie has worked the front desk for 11 years.").
8. **Nothing clever.** No device that needs a beat to decode ("patient reports", "the answer is an article"), no made-up concept words ("crust"), no figurative or regional phrasing. The market's own words from offers/<slug>/LANGUAGE.md and research.
9. **No throat-clearing after the hook.** Line 2 never comments on line 1 ("I know how that sounds"). Line 2 is what is in it for her.
10. **Every line escalates.** A scene line carries a hit. "There was a little H next to my liver number" is information; the doctor writing "patient reports one glass" in the chart is a hit.
11. **Digits for numbers.** 20 years, 13, $1,200, 60 points.
12. **The reveal belongs to the article.** The helper gives the quote and the stake; the mechanism and the organs come from the article paragraph, one to three lines, late, riding inside a feeling.
13. **A single NEVER in caps is personality.** One caps word on a feeling line, as seasoning. Never on nouns.

## 3. Hooks and openers under this rule

- A hook is one spoken sentence a person would say first at the kitchen table, 10 to 16 words, the twist word last. Fourth-grade words. Never two sentences in a short cold hook (Ryan, 10-28: "Never do two sentences in a hook. You have to talk like a human being. Otherwise it's confusing.").
- A video opener may be two sentences when the second lands the first, the way Maria's Camera Talk does (VID863, the TPS ad spending $228k a week): "If your jowls look like this and a dermatologist told you it's 'just your age,' look at these pictures of me. I'm 67, and I've never had a needle in my face." Who it is for, the brush-off she heard, the proof, the taboo she never did.
- Ryan's own ideal opener for a transformation video: "This is Benny 3 months ago, and this is his teeth now. No, he didn't go under. It wasn't brushing, it wasn't Greenies or water additives."
- A hook that needs the body to make sense is not a hook. If a stranger hearing only the hook could ask "why what?" or "the five what?", it is incomplete.

## 4. Before he sees it (every draft, every round)

1. Run `python3 tools/copygate/human_check.py <file>` and fix every flag: paragraphs over 40 words, fragments under 8 words that carry no feeling, runs of three short sentences, paragraphs with no connective, pronouns opening a paragraph.
2. Put his Benny paragraphs next to yours and ask which reads more like a person talking to her phone. If his, rewrite yours. Do not add words to his lines, do not trim them.
3. Read it aloud once. Any line you would never say to a friend across a table is wrong.
4. On a correction ("choppy", "chunky", "long-winded"), change only what he named and keep every liked line word for word. A rewrite that swings from fragments to 40-word paragraphs, or back, is the same mistake twice (11-03: "where I told you like 17 times not to do that, and you did it").

## 5. The failures he graded, so you recognize them

- "No, no, and no." / "His back teeth were brown." (fragments stacked for punch)
- "The $1,200 cleaning I was so scared of doesn't even touch, blah blah" (long-winded, no hit)
- "from a vet who said something I'd never heard in my life" (vague where the hardest fact goes)
- "Estrogen, an enzyme, five foods." (not how a human talks to a human)
- "shove 'cut back'", "Went normal WTF??" (non-human phrasing he had to rewrite himself)
- "Who I was getting pretty for?" / "And what did you mean, 40 years?" (lines a person would never say)
- "I know how that sounds." (throat-clearing after the hook)
- massive paragraphs after "not choppy, conversational" (conversational is the sentences, never the paragraph size)
