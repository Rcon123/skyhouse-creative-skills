# The Hook Laws
The guidelines for writing hooks that stop the scroll.
(Ryan's doctrine, verbatim, 2026-07-10. Required reading before writing any hook, ad, or headline.)

## RULE 0 — DEAD SIMPLE OR DEAD (Ryan, 2026-08-25, cannot be missed; wording PROVISIONAL until Ryan grades it)

If a hook is confusing, OR not relatable, OR not crystal clear, OR not specific: IT WON'T WORK. No exceptions, no matter how vicious or clever it is. "Like fucking DEAD simple."

This rule outranks voltage. Every other quality is multiplied by zero if this gate fails. The three kill tests, run on every hook before Ryan sees it:
1. COLD STRANGER TEST: someone with zero context must be able to repeat the hook's meaning back in one plain sentence. If the writer has to explain it, it is dead.
2. REAL BEHAVIOR TEST: every action, scene, or gesture in the hook must be something real people verifiably do, either documented in the research verbatim or so universal you could name three people who do it. Inventing a bodily mechanic or scene to "visualize" a documented feeling is how fake behaviors get in (killed example: "you hug your grandkids with your shoulders now" — nobody has ever heard of that being done).
3. PLAIN WORDS TEST: no metaphor, poetry, or abstraction doing the work of a fact (killed example: "the day you stopped fighting and started hiding"). Viciousness comes from a harder TRUE fact in plainer words, never from literary intensity.
4. EVERY DETAIL DOES A JOB TEST (PROVISIONAL, Ryan 2026-09-08, Testo wife-advice round 2): no texture details added for flavor ("F*cks me before coffee": "what does coffee have to do with anything?"). Every noun in the hook is either the qualifier, the before, the after, or the question. Spell it "Cum," never "come." The person she tells must be someone a real wife tells (sister, friend), never her daughter.

> **REQUIRED SECOND READ: `strategist-references/cumulative-feedback-protocol.md`** (Ryan's verbatim revision protocol, 2026-08-17). No hook session starts without it. The compressed core:
> 1. **Feedback is cumulative.** Every correction stays active. Never fix the newest complaint by regressing on an older one.
> 2. **Approved = LOCKED verbatim.** "This is good" freezes that wording. Change only what was criticized.
> 3. **Curiosity is ONE withheld reveal** surrounded by maximum concrete info (who, when, event type, who it happened to, why I care). Vague referents (it, they, something) only after the sentence has given them concrete meaning.
> 4. **Benefit early.** The viewer knows why they should care within the first sentence or two.
> 5. **Believable knowledge, natural.** The avatar needs a reason to know, but don't jam credentials into a hook; the NEXT line can do that job. Knowledge and motive are different things.
> 6. **Normal speech.** 4th-grade words, real spoken sentences. Never choppy compression.
> 7. **"Harder" = the most surprising specific fact, faster.** Never cleverer or more cryptic. **"Shorter" = cut filler, never concretes.**
> 8. **Never invent facts.** Source says it, research suggests it, or we inferred it: only the first goes in copy.
> 9. **Respect the funnel.** The hook sells the NEXT page only. Know the whole path before writing.
> 10. **The STOP rule.** If the hook is stronger ending here, end it. The body handles credibility, proof, story.
> 11. **Continuation rule.** A fixed second half continues the SAME thought (one-thought test); it never bolts on a new fact, character, or claim.
> 12. **Run the 14-point checklist** in the protocol file before showing anything.

## Luke's Loom laws (added 2026-08-11, from Luke's 2026-08-10 ad review; transcript: source/luke-loom-2026-08-10-hook-teachings.txt)

L1. **Filler + THE POINT.** Every hook is filler, filler, THE POINT. Filler must be dead simple and mechanical; the point must stand out so hard your eye lands on it ("damn, that's the thing"). If every phrase sits at the same level, the hook fails. Before shipping, name the hook's essence in one phrase; if you can't, it has no point.
L2. **Caveman + bigger moments.** One sentence (two allowed). Simpler words, shorter words, bigger moments. Hooks are poetry.
L3. **The information trade-off.** Specificity buys curiosity but costs comprehension. Every detail must earn its space; cut details that don't serve the point (the stove-barefoot rule).
L4. **The naming mechanism sets the curiosity ceiling.** "This weird Indonesian fruit" > "Amla." Always ask: what am I CALLING this thing to create maximum curiosity?
L5. **One power word per hook** (bullied, whipping, abusing) — but an image-verb used metaphorically fails when its literal picture overpowers the meaning (whipping your colon = the whip visual wins, meaning lost; "abusing" lands it).
L6. **Point first.** If the interesting part is buried mid-hook, flip it to the front. "Scientists just figured out" adds novelty on top.
L7. **Intuitive mechanism bridges.** Each mechanism step must FEEL true: "starving nerve → pain" doesn't connect; "starving nerve = your foot falling asleep, and the pain is the nerve screaming" connects. Then answer: why does THIS fix it better than anything else?
L8. **Intensify the contrast within compliance.** "Good thing doesn't help" is weak; "laxatives don't fix constipation, they keep you reliant… and in some cases lead to X" is the shape.
L9. **Free demographic tuning in word choice** (granddaughter vs daughter ages the narrator without a single extra word).
L10. **Market trigger words are real** (poop, for the gut market). Know yours.

## Ryan's graded corrections (2026-08-19, RJV/TPS/TFC yapper session) - PROVISIONAL until Ryan grades the wording

Y1. **Never tease the article in a hook.** Ryan: "I just don't like teasing the article in the hook. I think it's kinda weak and boring." Lines like "it took me one article to get mine back" or "she sent me a ranking" belong in the body. The hook can tease the transformation, the cause, or the reveal, never the destination page.

Y2. **Vague is anything that does not name the body part.** "I got thin and old in the same year" was graded vague on a hair offer: "I don't really know you're talking about hair." Every hook has to name the actual thing on the first read (the ponytail, the bald spot, the lines around my mouth, the sag under my jaw). An emotional summary is not a qualifier.

Y3. **Wordy dies before it grabs.** A hook that stacks a scene, a quote and a result across three clauses gets cut even when the scene is good. Front-load the point, then one short payoff, then stop.

Y4. **Photo-anchored hooks.** Ryan asked for a hook that literally opens on the damage on screen: "Look at this bald spot. This was me three months ago... now look at my hair." Put the photo full screen on the first word and cut back to her on the payoff. Every yapper batch should carry at least one of these.

Y6. **Name the drug, name the thing.** "The men on these shots" was graded vague. If a hook leans on a category (weight loss shots, injections, procedures), say the actual name the buyer says out loud. Ozempic, not "these shots."

Y7. **The photo reveal is the body opening, not a hook.** Ryan on the bald spot line: "I think 6 is the body opening." A hook that is really a visual reveal ("Look at this bald spot, this was me three months ago... now look at my hair") belongs in the first beat after the hook, running under every hook in the set, not competing with them. Every yapper brief should specify that opening beat in field 15.

Y8. **"Right direction but can hit harder" means find the harder fact in the same scene.** The hairdresser hook went from her announcing it to the salon (a moment) to her asking the narrator if she was on Ozempic because she could tell from her scalp (a professional reading the drug off her head). Same scene, harder fact, fewer words.

Y9. **"Harder and faster" never suspends the other laws.** The 08-19 TPS/TFC round-2 hooks got killed wholesale for exactly this: chasing punch, they dropped the qualifier from the first words ("Eleven thousand dollars. That is what a clinic wanted to fix my face" - fix what about it?) and opened on floating referents ("None of it ever worked" - none of WHAT?). A hook that hits harder by hitting vaguer is a dead hook. The qualifier law, the referent law and the name-the-thing law hold at every intensity.

Y5. **On accounts with heavy history, "okay" hooks are a fail.** Ryan on TPS and TFC: "we need something that's really fresh and new that's gonna really fucking grab them... we've done so much, and these are just kind of too basic." When the account has run hundreds of creatives, matching the winners' shape is not enough. Change what the scene compares her to, or what she is doing, not just the wording.

Y10. **The transformation payoff is social proof, plain, about the named body part.** (Ryan, 08-19 TPS session.) The after-line works best as something someone ELSE said or did about her face/hair, never her own claim and never an image that needs decoding ("I see my mother the year she met my father" and "she kissed both my cheeks goodnight" both failed as too-clever). His reference examples of the shape (not to be reused verbatim): a much younger guy hitting on her, the husband asking something, the ex's girlfriend asking who does her Botox. The payoff must pass the same 4th-grade clarity bar as the opener.

## Ryan's graded corrections (2026-08-11, UroControl hook session)

R1. **One sentence. Ellipsis over period.** A hook is one sentence. If it needs two thoughts, join them with an ellipsis... A period between two full sentences is the rare exception, not the default shape. The word after the ellipsis is capitalized, like a new sentence ("...kept me leaking for years... All 3 came from my doctor").
R2. **Confusion kills harder than politeness.** "Slap" means the reader gets hit AND gets it, instantly. An ugly true detail that needs backstory to parse (the mesh that ate through her mother's bladder) is not aggressive, it's confusing, and it dies. Never trade comprehension for viciousness.
R3. **No floating pronouns, no cold metaphors.** Every actor in the hook is named ("they knew" with no named they = dead). A metaphor that only lands after the body's setup ("pressed the nuclear button on your hormones") cannot open the ad. A stranger must picture every noun on the first read.
R4. **No every-time absolutes.** Don't hang a hook on a symptom frequency the buyer herself would dispute ("I sneezed and nothing happened" implies she leaked EVERY sneeze; most don't). State the symptom the way she'd describe it herself... the bank's winners say "kept coming back within hours," not "every single time." Check the ad's own body for what frequency it actually claims.
R5. **The aggression ceiling is her own vocabulary.** Escalate the ugly phrase only as far as the narrator would actually say it out loud. The proven ladder from the UroControl session: "leaking" (clinical, vanilla) → "peeing my pants at 61" → "wetting myself in public" → "put me in diapers at 61" (the market's nightmare word about themselves) → "sitting in a puddle of my own pee at my granddaughter's recital" (max, needs the scene to itself). One rung past her register ("pissed myself" from a churchgoing 61-year-old) and believability snaps. Aggression that needs decoding is out (R2); aggression past her voice is out (this law); everything between is fair game.

*These aren't commandments. They're the patterns that show up across the hooks that actually print money. Use them as a lens, not a checklist. Break one on purpose if you know why you're breaking it.

2. Qualify your reader in the first 3-5 words.
The reader should know who this ad is for within the first few words. The category word, or an unmistakable symptom of the problem, wants to be right at the start, not buried in the middle or tucked at the end.
Someone scrolling at 10pm, half-paying attention, is deciding in about three seconds whether to stop. If the qualifier hits them early, you've earned the rest of the hook. If it doesn't, they're already gone.

3. Write for a fourth grader.
Confusion is the single biggest reason hooks die. If any part of your hook makes the reader think "wait, what?", it's probably dead.
Don't assume they'll connect dots. Don't reference brands, places, or concepts not everyone knows. If your own brain even slightly hurts reading it, it's worth a rewrite. Clarity almost always beats cleverness.

6. The first few words have to grab.
The first three to five words are the most valuable real estate in the entire ad. Their only job is to stop the scroll.
Skip the credentials, the setup, the warm-up. "I've been a dermatologist for 20 years" is dead weight. "Ladies, I need to confess something" is dead weight. Front-load the intrigue.

7. Sound like a real person, not an ad.
If it sounds like writing, rewrite it until it sounds like talking. Use contractions. Use fragments. RHYTHM in your copy. Start sentences with "And," "But," "So," "Look." Write to one person, like you're talking to a friend.
The moment it smells like marketing copy, or worse, like AI, the reader is gone.

9. Put quotes around one word, the most provocative one.
Strategic quotes create a pause and pull the eye. "Secret" Botox makes the reader stop on that word. "Secret Botox" doesn't.
Pick the single most provocative word in the hook and put it in quotes. One per hook, never more.

10. Capitalize the keywords that need to pop.
Capitalize the important keywords, not every word, just the ones you want the eye to catch first. It makes the hook skimmable and gives the scene visual weight.

11. Use ellipses for pacing.
Ellipses (…) create rhythm. They give the reader a beat before a payoff lands. They make copy easier on the eye and build little moments of suspense. Used well, they're a cheat code.

12. Never use em dashes.
No em dashes in a hook. Ever. They read as AI-generated and they break the conversational voice. Use ellipses instead if you need a pause.

13. Build around pain points your reader actually says out loud.
If the pain isn't something a real customer has said verbatim, in a forum, a review, a survey, a conversation, there's a good chance it's copywriter imagination.
Stop inventing pains because they "sound evocative." The people living with the problem already know what matters to them, and the list is shorter than you think. Use their actual words, not yours.

14. If you tease a transformation, make it big and specific.
Abstract verbs like "initiated," "felt better," or "went hiking" don't land. They're small and vague. Show the SIZE of the change.
"He ran a 5K on Saturday." "He built my daughter's crib last month." "She cried when he touched her." Things you can see, with scale attached. Match the weight of the after to the weight of the before.

16. Go for deep emotion, not surface.
The shallow version of a pain point rarely hits. "He's tired." "She's frustrated." "He's not sleeping." It reads as small because it IS small. It's the symptom, not the stakes.
The deep version is about what they've lost. Identity. Confidence. Their marriage. Who they used to be. Who they can't be anymore. That's what makes someone click. Not the symptom, the stakes underneath it.

17. Strong hooks usually tease a payoff.
The reader should feel there's something worth clicking for on the other side. Something juicy waiting. "I HAVE to know more," not "okay, and?"
It doesn't always have to be a literal result. It can be a revelation, a story beat, a surprise. But a flat observation with nothing waiting behind it almost never converts.

18. Pass the skeptic test.
Read your hook out loud. Would a cynical stranger dismiss it with "yeah, sure"? If yes, it probably needs a rework.
The audience has seen everything. Direct benefit claims don't work anymore. Story, contradiction, mechanism tease, identity: all of these sneak past the skeptic in ways a flat promise can't.

19. Don't tell them what they already know.
If your reader has been dealing with their problem for years, they've heard the basics a thousand times. Lecturing them about their own problem is wallpaper. They scroll right past.
The hook should feel NEW or SURPRISING to someone who's lived with this forever. If it's common knowledge in the niche, it's not really a hook.

21. The reader is the hero.
Not the product. Not the expert. Not the narrator. The reader.
Every hook should make them feel this is about me, this could be me, this is for me. If the product or the expert is the star of the show, the reader has no seat in the story, and they scroll.

22. Never copy a winning ad. Steal the principle, not the words.
Winning ads are for learning, never for copying. Study them to understand why they worked: the pattern, the structure, the emotional beat. Then go write something genuinely new.
Copying a winner is just handing someone back something they already have. That's not creative work, it's regurgitation, and every trained eye spots it instantly.

23. Only "holy shit" is good enough.
"Not bad" isn't good enough. "Pretty good" isn't good enough. "Okay" isn't good enough.
The bar is "holy shit, I have to know more." If you're not reacting to your own hook that way, the reader definitely won't. Be your own harshest critic, or the market will do it for you.
