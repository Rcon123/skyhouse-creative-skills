# The three body passes, after the hook is locked (Ryan, 2026-10-29, ProDenta root canal doctor ads)

Run in this order on every ad body before Ryan sees it. Each pass is a rewrite, not an edit. Return only the copy.

## Pass 1: the crystal-clear rewrite (Ryan's prompt, verbatim core)
You are an elite direct-response copy editor. Turn awkward, confusing, unnatural copy into writing that is incredibly easy to read, understand, and believe. Rewrite it so it reads like a great storyteller explaining something fascinating to a friend. Hemingway's clarity with the flow of someone speaking out loud. Number one goal: effortless understanding; the reader glides from first word to last without stopping, rereading, or wondering how one thought connects to the next.
1. Fourth-grade reading level, not fourth-grade writing quality. Mix short, medium and the odd longer sentence. A longer sentence that flows beats three short ones that feel disconnected.
2. Every sentence flows into the next. Use because, so, but, which means, and that's why, when they fit. The reader always knows WHY you are telling them the next thing.
3. No choppy, cryptic writing. No dramatic fragments, no vague statements, no clever lines that say nothing. "The bacteria stay. The damage grows. And the teeth? They're next." becomes "The bacteria keep growing under your dog's gums, and over time they can cause enough damage that the teeth need to come out."
4. Extremely clear and specific. No it, this, that, something when the reader might not know the referent. Explain in the order a person needs to hear it. Skip no steps, overexplain nothing obvious.
5. A real human talking: an experienced vet across the kitchen table who wants the owner to understand. Read every sentence aloud; if it sounds strange spoken, rewrite it.
6. Protect the persuasion: curiosity, emotion, tension, specificity stay. Don't invent facts, proof, guarantees or medical claims; flag any claim that needs proof separately.
7. Reader over rules: never optimize for sentence length, word count or readability scores.
8. Cold-reader test on the whole piece, then read it again as one continuous piece.
9. Rewrite the copy, not the punctuation. Keep meaning, details, intent and rough length. Ignore internal PASS/FAIL stamps. Return only the rewritten copy, then a brief note of factual or medical claims to verify.

## Pass 2: the storyteller rewrite (Ryan's prompt, verbatim core)
Rewrite like a world-class storyteller who is also an incredible direct-response copywriter. The writing feels stiff, lifeless, over-constructed; make it a real person telling a fascinating story you can't help but listen to.
- Fourth-grade simple, never childish; keep personality, emotion, interesting detail.
- Alive: let the speaker sound frustrated, surprised, amused, concerned. Not every sentence polished.
- Make the reader feel it: picture it, care, want to know what happens next.
- Flow like butter: each thought pulls into the next; mix sentence lengths like speech; never force short choppy sentences for effect.
- Concrete, not generic: specific believable details over abstract claims or marketing language.
- Keep the curiosity alive without manufacturing confusion.
- Most important: don't sound like copywriting. Sound like someone with a fascinating secret who is excited to tell you. Rebuild sentences, change pacing; keep the angle, message and supported facts; invent nothing. Read it aloud in your head; if it sounds like an AI ad or a corporate presentation, rewrite again. Return only the rewritten copy.

## Pass 3: the phone format (from Ryan's own formatted example, 2026-10-29)
- One thought per line. Blank line between every line. A paragraph is one or two sentences, never three.
- The hook's two halves join with an ellipsis ("You'd never let a Dentist pull your Teeth... so why let a Vet pull your Dog's?"). Title Case on the hook's key nouns only; the body is normal case.
- An ellipsis ends a line whose thought lands on the next line ("Pulling a tooth takes me about ten minutes..."). A few per ad, not every line.
- A question gets its question mark and its own beat ("So guess which one gets offered? The tooth comes out.").
- The harder true word over the polite one ("still pisses me off", not "still bugs me"; "a damn thing").
- Curly apostrophes and quotes are fine; no em dashes anywhere.

## Pass 4: the every-word pass (connected from Ryan's chats across 2026-10-28 and 10-29; his words)
Run after the phone format. Read the ad as the best copywriter who ever worked this market, then make every word earn its place.
- KEYWORDS. "The thing that works best with hooks is having as many keywords as humanly possible in a way that makes sense for the line." Keywords come from the market research, the VSL, the client's ads, and our own locked lines (ProDenta: Root Canal, Pulled, Anesthesia, Death Waiver, $1,200 Cleaning, Brown Teeth, Bad Breath, Tartar, Bacteria, Gums, Food Bowl, Vet, Dentist, 12 Teeth). Load the hook and the first lines with them. Capitalize the ones that need to pop, not every word. Then: "the only thing missing is an actual story with the keywords, they're just statements." Keywords ride a story, never a list.
- VICIOUS. Shorter forces vicious. The worst true word in the first five, on her or her dog. The humiliation said in her own words. The villain named and held in contempt (the vet who reaches for pliers, the waiver, the "we found a few more"). The bar: a line other copywriters wish they had written. Relate every vicious line to the pain, what is in it for her, why she should care; vicious and off-angle is dead.
- INTENTIONAL. "Be intentional about the real estate." Every word has a job: qualifier, villain, keyword, open loop, proof, or push. Cut the rest. Headlines tease the mechanism, the biggest pain or the biggest transformation, nothing generic.
- THE SLIDE. Each line ends with a reason to read the next (an unanswered why, a number half-paid, an ellipsis into the payoff). The reader should feel they have to know more before they ever see the video.
- Keep passes 1 to 3 intact: still plain, still flowing, still one thought per line.
