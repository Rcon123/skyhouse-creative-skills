FUNNEL RULES

###IMPORTANT: On a VSL offer the page is product-blind. Never name the product. Never show a price, bottle, discount, guarantee, or the word "buy." The page sells the video. The video sells the product.###

- Never promise the fix is free. Never write "just cut these foods," "no pills," or "that's all you have to do." The video sells a supplement. Say instead: "it starts with," "what to do about it," "the way he shows his patients."
- Hold the reveal. The fix stays hidden on the page. Tease it with a name. Never teach it.

RESEARCH RULES
Accuracy beats cleverness every time.
- Every pain, fear, scene, and behavior must be something real people said or verifiably do. If it is not in the research verbatim, or so common you could name three people who do it, it does not go in.
- Use the buyer's own words. Mechanism words come from the client's ads and VSL, never invented. Never coin a nickname a stranger has to decode.
- Lead with the biggest fear by count, not the most interesting one.
- Relatability check: the door into the page is the common symptom a third of the audience already has. The odd symptom is curiosity on top, never the qualifier.
- Qualify by the problem, never by a past action. ("You lost the weight but..." gates out everyone who never dieted.)
- Personas are 65+ unless the research says otherwise. Age the whole life: grandkids, fixed income, living alone, the pill organizer, the dog that follows her room to room.
- Never invent facts, stats, studies, or doctor quotes. A real doctor or vet only says what is in the client's VSL.
- Use the client's real numbers. If the client says $1,200, the page says $1,200.

THE VOICE

Sentence-level rules
- Plain words a 67-year-old says out loud. 4th-grade words. If a stranger has to read a line twice, it is wrong.
- Full, natural, conversational sentences with the connecting words in them ("because," "so," "which means"). Never robotic staccato like "That's not metaphor. That's basic physiology." If two sentences in a row are under six words, join them.
- Short paragraphs: one idea per line, one or two sentences per paragraph, never more than 3 lines on a phone. "Not choppy" refers to sentences, never to paragraph size. Long paragraphs are unreadable.
- Referent rule: every line names its thing. Never "it," "they," "the form," "the counter" without the sentence itself saying what. Restate the thing by name every time.
- Specific, never vague. Real numbers ($1,200, 12 teeth, 24 hours, 43 years, 5 seconds). Real credentials. Real brands she has tried (Greenies, Fosamax, Ozempic).
- Hardest true version, first try. Say the ugly word (dying, diapers, rotting, pulled). No hedging (may, might, some, generally).
- Vicious comes from a harder TRUE fact in plainer words, never from literary intensity.

Kill list: never use any of these, ever
- Em dashes, under any circumstances
- Metaphors or analogies
- Poetic abstractions
- "This isn't X, it's Y"
- "Not just X, but Y"
- "The truth is:" / "Picture this:" / "Here's the thing"
- Three-part lists as a default
- However / Moreover / Furthermore
- Jargon: leverage, optimize, game-changing, journey, delve, or any word not in the client's words list

Human patterns: use these
- Vary sentence length across the page
- Start sentences with And / But / So
- A conversational aside in parentheses
- Clear opinions stated directly
- A sudden question mid-copy
- Casual profanity only where it lands naturally
- Every line that asks something ends in a question mark

HEADLINES AND HOOKS

Headline construction rules
- One sentence. Join a second beat with an ellipsis or a parenthetical, never two separate sentences.
- Qualify the reader in the first 3 to 5 words (the body part, the symptom, the category).
- Credibility opens the headline ("43-Year Vet:", "NBA Doctor:"). The payoff is stated plainly, never implied.
- ONE quoted word (usually the nickname), ONE caps word max, a number or time stamp, often a parenthetical: (This), (No Anesthesia), (Try Tonight).
- Nickname grammar: [the thing she owns] + [a dead-simple machine word]: "Prostate Flush," "Food Bowl Trick," "Energy Switch." Never anatomy jargon. Never an unrelated object.
- Never spend the reveal after a colon.

Proven headline shapes
- Authority Stunned + nickname: "Vets Stunned: ... 'Food Bowl' Trick"
- Symptom question + nickname fix
- Authority command: "Dermatologist: If You Have Dark Spots, Do (This) Immediately"
- Enemy betrayal
- Spectacle watch: "Watch Her Age-Spots 'Dissolve'"
- Contradiction: "Wrinkles? STOP Washing Your Face"

Other headline rules
- Benefit beats diagnosis. The headline promises the visible after (brown teeth to white, the dog never goes under), not a generic "clean teeth."
- No $ signs in a squeeze page H1. No ellipses in a squeeze page H1. Short. No "and" stacked claims.

Hook laws
- Dead simple or dead.
- Cold stranger test: would a cold stranger who has never seen the offer understand it instantly?
- Real behavior test: is every detail something real people actually do?
- Plain words test: could a 67-year-old say it out loud without stumbling?
- Every detail does a job. Name the one point and make it land.
- "Harder" means the most surprising specific fact, delivered faster.
- "Shorter" means cut filler, never concretes.
