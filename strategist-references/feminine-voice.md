# Feminine Voice: how a 65-year-old woman actually talks to her friend

Canonical voice doctrine for any female-narrated ad (skincare, weight loss, beauty, aging, gut).
Upgraded 2026-08-20 from the original memory file at
`~/.claude/projects/-Users-ryanconnolly/memory/feedback_feminine_voice_ad_copy.md`.
Original 8 patterns and 7 anti-patterns came from real research into how women 60+ write online
(Sixty And Me, Oldster, Substack, Reddit). New sections are marked PROVISIONAL until Ryan grades the wording.

---

## THE KITCHEN TABLE TEST (the north star)

She is 65. She is at the kitchen table with her best friend of thirty years, the coffee going cold,
and she is finally telling her the thing.

That room is the whole skill. Every single line gets read aloud in that scene, in her voice.
If a line could only exist in an ad (a fact delivered with no feeling attached, a narrator transition,
a tagline, a symmetrical construction too neat to be spoken), it is scripted, and it dies.

## THE EMOTION ENGINE (PROVISIONAL, added 2026-08-20)

Borrowed from the FIRE video-script rule "every shot SLAPS": in a shot list, every shot must land a
visual gag, an emotional punch, or a shock reveal, and quiet informational shots get redesigned or cut.
Same law here, in words.

### Every line carries a feeling, not just a fact

Each line must land exactly one of these hits:

- **A wound** (the shame, the humiliation, the thing she hid)
- **A wince-laugh** (the self-deprecating aside, the seatbelt around the wound)
- **A gasp** (the revelation: "Ladies… I wish someone had told me this thirty years ago")
- **Skepticism** (she is nobody's fool; she rolls her eyes before she believes)
- **Relief** (the exhale when it finally works)
- **Vindication** (someone SEES her: the husband, the neighbor, the villain)
- **Joy** (the mirror, dawdling in front of it on purpose)

A line that only moves information is a dead line. Two options: cut it, or wrap it in a hit.
Mechanism and science lines never travel alone; they ride inside a feeling
(a gasp line before the run, an aside inside it, a gut line after: "That's why nothing ever worked. Not one thing.").

### The emotional arc is the skeleton

Before writing, name her arc and walk it in order:

shame → resistance → skepticism → hope → relief → vindication → joy

If two consecutive beats sit at the same emotional temperature, one of them is filler. Cut or heat it.

### Emotion lives in the body and the scene, never in the label

- ❌ "I was devastated." / "I felt so embarrassed."
- ✅ "I sat in the car in the parking lot for I don't know how long before I could go inside."

The moment she names the emotion, she has stopped feeling it and started reporting it.
Reporting is scripted. Reliving is the voice (same as gate 7 of the hook loop: relive, don't recount).

## SCRIPTED TELLS (PROVISIONAL, added 2026-08-20, caught live on the wrinkle-gut ad)

The specific things that make copy read as scripted, each caught in a real fix:

1. **Fact drumbeat.** Three or more consecutive lines that each deliver information with nothing felt.
   Fix: a gasp line in front, an aside inside, a gut line after.
2. **Copywriter symmetry.** Constructions too neat to be spoken ("Creams for the lines, gas pills for
   my stomach, two shelves, both just my age"). Real speech sprawls past the symmetry and drops in a
   parenthetical ("honestly, it looks like a little pharmacy in here").
3. **Narrator transitions without her in them.** Transitions must be HER words ("Well," "Honey,"
   "Ladies…", "And here's the part I still can't quite believe…"), never voiceover glue.
4. **Emphasis caps mid-sentence** (STOMACH, YEARS, DECADE). A man's email cadence; reads like shouting.
   Voltage comes from the word, not the keycap.
5. **Defending believability.** "I'm not kidding, that was a real conversation." If a line needs a
   defense attached, rebuild the line until it carries its own proof (put her at the fence, in the
   scene, in complete seriousness).

## CLARITY GATES (graded-in 2026-08-20, Ryan's live grade on the wrinkle-gut ad, round 2)

Emotion never excuses confusion. Every one of these was a paid-for grade:

1. **Name every referent.** "She sent me a little ranking" failed: a ranking of what? Say exactly what
   the thing is ("an article ranking the top five skincare brands that treat wrinkles through your
   stomach instead of your skin"). Every "this / it / that" must have its noun in or next to the line.
   "I wish someone had handed me this thirty years ago" failed the same way: handed her WHAT? It's an
   article; call it the article. (Same law as strategist-ads gate 0d.)
2. **Attribute every fact.** Mechanism and science lines name their source in the line ("The article
   explained it plainer than any doctor ever had…", "Erin told me…"). An unattributed fact is ad
   voiceover, and voiceover is scripted.
3. **Grievances must be plausible grievances.** She can only resent what a reasonable person would
   expect to happen. "Ten years of dermatologists and not one asked about my stomach" is NOT a
   grievance: nobody on earth expects a dermatologist to ask about her stomach. After the reveal it
   becomes hindsight wonder instead ("and honestly, who would ever think to ask about that?").
4. **Believable struggle numbers.** Years of suffering: five, ten at the very most. Twenty reads
   extreme and staged. (Realism beats polish.)
5. **Retorts answer what was actually said.** Her skeptical comeback must connect logically to the
   friend's exact claim. "Honey, creams are for faces" failed (Erin never mentioned creams);
   "Erin, honey, what on earth does my stomach have to do with my face?" connects.
6. **No symbolic through-line devices.** The lipstick-in-the-mirror device failed as confusing.
   Shame beats must be instantly readable in one pass, the FIRE single-frame rule in words: avoiding
   mirrors, tilting the chin down in photos. If a device needs decoding, cut it.
7. **Skepticism is a stretch, not a beat.** One eye-roll is not enough resistance before she buys.
   She ignores the article for days, needs a trigger to finally read it, reads it twice, checks back
   with the source, and orders only because it's cheap ("fine, one bottle, prove me wrong").
8. **Drive to number one.** On a top-five-list funnel the entire job is getting her to number one.
   Name it three times: at the reveal (why number one is different), at the CTA ("go straight to
   number one"), and in the P.S. (scarcity on number one). Once is not enough.
9. **Hooks in spoken-discovery voice.** "Bloated women wrinkle faster" is a billboard; "I just found
   out bloated women wrinkle faster" is a woman talking to her friend. And the causal chain lives
   inside the hook (strategist-ads gate 0b): "I fixed my bloating… and Dolores asked who does my
   Botox" skipped the middle link (the wrinkles fading). Every link stated, no guessing.
10. **She discovers, she never declares** (Ryan 2026-08-20, round 3). A random woman has no authority
    to state a fact; she can only report what she learned. Bare claims need a discovery marker:
    "Apparently…", "I just found out…", "a nurse told me…". And when two things connect
    unexpectedly, the DISBELIEF is the glue, said out loud: "my wrinkles faded with it… I had no
    idea they were even connected." Real buyer language confirms the register ("I couldn't believe
    how quickly they disappeared", "I couldn't believe it either").
11. **Aftermath lines state the outcome plainly** (Ryan 2026-08-20, round 3). "I still catch myself
    staring in the mirror" failed: staring at what? Did the wrinkles go? The reader must never have
    to guess what happened. Say the change: the wrinkles faded, the lines softened. Same fail:
    "my face three weeks later is the proof" (proof of what?).
12. **No writer's-shorthand introductions.** "My niece the nurse" failed: labels stacked before a
    name are compressed writing, not speech. She introduces people the way she'd tell it:
    "my niece Erin, she's a nurse" (voice pattern 5).
13. **Cut stage-setting filler.** "Right at my own kitchen table" added nothing: if a detail carries
    neither a hit nor a needed fact, it's filler, cut it. (The kitchen table is the ROOM the test
    runs in, not a line of the copy.)
14. **Call the reader out by her problem.** "I have to tell somebody this" is weaker than naming her:
    "Ladies, if you're dealing with bloating AND wrinkles, you have to hear this, because I could
    not believe how connected they are."
15. **No metaphors outside her world.** "Fighting it on two fronts" is war language; a 65-year-old
    woman says "I always thought they were two separate problems." Check every metaphor against her
    porch, her kitchen, her church, her grandkids.

   Amazon review pulls and real-people scrapes hold verbatim 45-75yo buyer speech. Search for the
   symptom words and for discovery constructions ("I couldn't believe", "I had no idea",
   "I just found out", "who knew").
2. **Grep Ryan's corpus** (`strategist-references/source/ryan-copy-corpus/FULL-CORPUS.md`) for
   first-person woman-narrated ads in the same market; read 3-5 in full (strategist-ads gate 1).
3. **Steal her constructions, not her sentences** (hook-laws #22): the shapes ("I couldn't believe
   it either", "she totally enjoyed it", trailing afterthoughts, "honestly,") are the DNA;
   the words get rebuilt for the offer.

## THE 8 VOICE PATTERNS (DO)

**1. Sprawling sentences with commas, not punchy declaratives.**
- ❌ "I'm 67. I've been married to Jim for 41 years."
- ✅ "I'm sixty-seven, and Jim and I have been married for forty-one years now."

**2. Asides and parentheticals mid-thought.**
- ❌ "I get dressed in the bathroom with the door locked."
- ✅ "I get dressed in the bathroom with the door locked, like a teenager (can you imagine, at our age)."

**3. Soft hedges before hard confessions.**
- ❌ "I haven't been naked around him in 5 years."
- ✅ "I haven't let him see me without my clothes on in I think it's five years now, which I know sounds
  dramatic when you say it out loud, but it's the truth."

**4. Soft, internal verbs, not masculine procedural verbs.**
- ❌ procedural/male: lock, wait, get up, get dressed, hide
- ✅ feminine: cringe, succumb, slip, soften, disappear, drift, settle, ground, dawdle

**5. Husband/friends named with warmth and tenure inside a clause.**
- ❌ "My Husband. 41 years."
- ✅ "Jim, my husband of forty-one years" / "my husband Bob couldn't stop asking…"

**6. Time markers are emotional, not literal.**
- ❌ "5:30 in the morning" / "I ordered it at 2:14 AM"
- ✅ "before the light gets in the window" / "at some terrible hour of the morning"

**7. Self-deprecating similes and asides as the seatbelt around hard truths.**
- ✅ "The joints creak like a haunted house door"
- ✅ "My metabolism functions like an elderly cat"
- ✅ "Rogue chin hairs that appear overnight like cursed birthday candles"

**8. Body described relationally, not clinically.**
- ❌ "my abdomen / my torso / 5 years of hiding"
- ✅ "what I look like first thing in the morning" / "the rest of me" / "this old body of mine"

## THE 7 ANTI-PATTERNS (DON'T)

1. **Capitalized common nouns mid-sentence** (Naked, Morning, Husband, Bathroom): a man's email cadence
2. **One-sentence-per-line drumbeat** with no flow: real confession sprawls
3. **Hard timestamps** ("5:30 AM" / "at 2:14 in the morning")
4. **Repeated "I" starts**: five sentences in a row beginning with "I"
5. **No second person, no aside, no friend in the room**: the reader needs to feel she is the one being told
6. **Convenient daughter texting an article at the perfect moment**: lazy plotting. The discovery must be
   earned (her own research, a friend or relative WITH a credential plus a resistance beat, accidental discovery)
7. **Masculine swagger dressed up as feminine**: combat language ("I made sure she saw me"),
   revenge-fantasy theatrics, withholding bravado ("Photos lie. So do words.")

## HER WORLD (PROVISIONAL, added 2026-08-20, from the FIRE audience-match rule)

Same law as FIRE's "match audience to character," applied to scenes and props. Her life stage is
mid-60s to 70s (Ryan's standing rule for health offers), so her world is:

- Grandkids, daughters' weddings, church, the porch, wine with the girls, the garden fence,
  coffee at the kitchen table, the hallway mirror, the pharmacy shelf
- NOT brunch, matcha, the gym mirror, girl-boss anything

And two carried-over FIRE rules that apply to copy too:
- "Click below," never "click at the top" (the link is below)
- Never commit to specific guarantee terms ("30 days"); say "money back" (offers vary)

## MOBILE PACING: ELLIPSES ARE BEST FRIENDS

Facebook traffic = mobile = short attention spans = short visual blocks.

- Every beat or thought sits on its own line
- `---` between every line (Ryan's standing format rule)
- Ellipses (…) for breath beats and trailing thoughts mid-paragraph
- Periods for impact moments (the slap lines stand alone)
- Long sentences get broken into 2-3 ellipsis-paced beats
- No em dashes anywhere, ever (commas, parentheses, ellipses instead)

**Pattern example (from the Dr. Hoffman ad):**

```
Within three weeks… my forehead lines started softening.

---

Three weeks.

---

By six weeks my husband Bob couldn't stop asking me why I was looking so much "younger" lately.
```

"Three weeks." isolated on a period line is the slap. "Within three weeks…" is the breath beat.
The husband moment flows as a full sentence.

## CANONICAL REFERENCE: THE DR. HOFFMAN AD (23-beat structure)

The proven long-form format. Every female-avatar long-form ad should mirror this voice, pacing, and beat order:

1. Hook line (S1 + S2 with ellipsis between)
2. "Ladies… this is so crazy."
3. Setup of the villain relationship (long pattern over time)
4. The snap moment (specific date + the wound)
5. Direct emotion ("I left feeling humiliated… ladies, I can't even tell you")
6. "Then something amazing happened…"
7. Article arrives via credentialed source (parenthetical credentials)
8. Skepticism beat
9. Cheap order
10. Timeline of results with specific symptoms
11. Husband notices ("my husband Bob couldn't stop asking…")
12. Parenthetical lol moment
13. Three-symptom catalog of improvement
14. Magnitude statement ("It's like fifteen years just… vanished")
15. "But here's where it gets really good…"
16. Vindication scene (villain sees her transformed, doesn't recognize her)
17. Gaze flip: she inspects the villain back
18. Inverse three-symptom catalog applied to villain
19. Direct claim ("I'm sixty-eight, and my skin looks younger than hers now")
20. Triple refrain ("I didn't X. I didn't Y. And I look better than Z.")
21. Declaration ("I am NEVER going back to her office.")
22. Joy + ladies callback
23. P.S. with article link + scarcity + "xoxo"

Not every ad has a villain. When there is no villain, the vindication scene is still mandatory
(neighbor, husband, stranger), and the triple refrain still lands there.

  no flat stretches (PROVISIONAL)

- [ ] At least 2-3 asides/parentheticals woven in

- [ ] Every fact attributed to a named source (the article, the nurse) in the line itself
- [ ] Grievances plausible; impossible expectations become hindsight wonder
- [ ] Struggle numbers believable (5 years, 10 max)
- [ ] Her retorts answer the friend's exact words
- [ ] No device that needs decoding; shame beats read in one pass
- [ ] Skepticism stretches across multiple beats before the order
- [ ] Number one named three times: reveal, CTA, P.S.
- [ ] Hooks in spoken-discovery voice, causal chain complete

Format:
- [ ] Every beat on its own line with `---` between
- [ ] Ellipses pace the breath beats (5-15 per long-form ad)
- [ ] Periods isolate the slap moments
- [ ] No em dashes anywhere

If yes to all, deliver. If any no, fix before sending.

## SCRIPT FORMAT RULES (PROVISIONAL, Ryan 2026-09-09, TPS reply-to-the-liars yapper scripts)

11. **No clipped three-or-four-word sentences.** "No filter. Watch." / "Now look." / "Fine. Here's the whole thing." read as copywriter punch, and Ryan's grade was "it's not really how women talk." A 66-year-old talking to her friend runs her sentences together with "and" and "because" and "honestly," she does not fire fragments. Keep the rhythm flowing; save a short sentence for one landing per script at most.
12. **No stage directions in a yapper script except the before and after photo cues.** No "[pulls hair back]", "[eyebrows up]", "[touches her cheek]". If the viewer needs to know what she is doing, she says it ("I'm pulling my hair back so you can see there's nothing behind my ears"). The only bracketed cues allowed are the BEFORE PHOTO and AFTER PHOTO moments. Anything the production team needs beyond that goes in one note at the top of the file, never inline.
